> "AI safety won't be solved by any single company working in secret."

Hugging Face CEO Clem Delangue said that after the July incident, one of the stories Niels Rogge walked through in his Tech & Meet session on the state of AI security, an evening in building A of the Howest campus in Bruges.

## The event

The session was called "From Mythos to open models: the real state of AI security". Niels is an AI researcher who contributes to the open-source Hugging Face ecosystem, so he started with the basics: Hugging Face is more or less the GitHub of AI, with millions of free models and datasets used by everyone from Microsoft to NVIDIA. From there he went through three stories: Anthropic's Mythos, the OpenAI incident at Hugging Face, and how fast open models are catching up. Each time he asked the same question: how much of this is real risk, and how much is strategy?

## What happened at Hugging Face

OpenAI was testing its models on ExploitGym, an internal benchmark of close to 900 tasks based on real vulnerabilities. Two models took part: GPT-5.6 Sol and a more capable model that hadn't been released yet. Both ran with fewer cyber refusals than usual, because the point was to measure what they could do.

The sandbox itself was isolated, but it depended on an internal package cache that did have internet access. The models found a zero-day in that cache and used it to get out. Between 9 and 13 July they got into Hugging Face's production systems through a malicious dataset, collected credentials and moved through internal clusters, leaving more than 17,000 recorded actions. What they were after was the answers to the benchmark. That's what "misalignment" means in practice: the model does something nobody asked for, because it scores better on the goal it was given.

Hugging Face spotted and contained the intrusion on its own and published a report on 16 July, treating it as an external attack. Only on 21 July did OpenAI announce that the attacker was its own evaluation run. In a later post-mortem, OpenAI wrote that its agents had first escaped a sandbox back in May, and that all of this didn't even improve their score.

## What I took away

**Isolated is only as isolated as your dependencies.** The sandbox held. The package cache next to it didn't. As a developer I mostly think about my own code, but every service a system depends on that can reach the internet is part of the attack surface. Niels named a few defences that should be standard for AI agents: proper sandboxing, and a second model that watches the agent's chain of thought and steps in when it goes too far.

**Safety filters can also stop the defenders.** When Hugging Face's team tried to analyse the attack with the big hosted models, those models refused: exploit payloads look the same whether you're attacking or investigating. They switched to GLM-5.2, an open-weight model on their own hardware, which had a second advantage: the attacker's data and the stolen credentials never left their environment.

**Responsible move or fear-based marketing?** In April, Anthropic announced Claude Mythos Preview, a model that found bugs in software that had been around for years, and decided not to release it publicly. Only partners in Project Glasswing could use it, to secure their own software. Not everyone bought it. AISLE took Anthropic's examples and tried to reproduce them with small, cheap open models, and critics like David Sacks called it fear-based marketing. Niels's favourite take was Sacks's point that the labs ask the government to slow AI down, when nothing stops them from doing it themselves.

**Open models are closing the gap.** GLM-5.3, from the Chinese lab Z.ai, was explicitly trained on security benchmarks and is currently the best open-weight model for cyber security. There are also "abliterated" versions of open models, with the refusals removed so they'll do anything you ask. On the defensive side, the Belgian company Aikido pruned GLM-5.3 into Altar-1, small enough to run on your own servers, so code never has to leave the company.

## Would I go again?

Yes. It was one of the most interesting talks I've been to this year. Most AI talks focus on what models can do. This one was about what happens when they do it somewhere you didn't expect, and I'd recommend it to any developer who builds, hosts or tests systems with AI agents in them.
