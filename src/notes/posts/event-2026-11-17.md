> "Climate AI ≠ generative AI."

That line was stamped across one of Wim Casteels's slides during his Tech & Meet session "Het masker van AI" ("The mask of AI"), an evening in building A of the Howest campus in Bruges. It sums up the whole talk: "AI" is one word for very different things, and you can only use it well if you know which one you're dealing with.

## The event

Wim is a lecturer and researcher in IT & AI at AP Hogeschool in Antwerp. Before that he was an AI researcher at IDLab (imec and the University of Antwerp) and at the Royal Meteorological Institute, and a data scientist at Argenta. The talk shares its title with his book, which maps out the many ways AI affects society. It wasn't meant as a technical deep dive. The goal was a critical framework for using AI responsibly.

He started on 30 November 2022, the day ChatGPT appeared as a simple website where anyone could ask a question. It reached 100 million users in two months. From there he went back to 1956, when a summer workshop gave the field its name, and through the waves that followed: rule-based AI that didn't live up to the hype, expert systems in the eighties, machine learning in the nineties (think of your spam filter), deep learning and computer vision in the 2010s, and now chatbots. The rest of the evening covered superintelligence, the planet, Europe and what you can do yourself.

## What I took away

**"AI" in a press release can mean two very different things.** Faster weather forecasts, balancing the power grid, discovering new materials, tracking deforestation: that's mostly traditional machine learning, which is small and efficient. The big energy bill comes from generative AI. Wim pointed to [The AI Climate Hoax](https://algorithmwatch.org/en/ai-climate-hoax/), a report from February 2026 that found 74% of Big Tech's claims about AI's climate benefits are unproven, and that most of the benefits that are claimed come from traditional AI. One type of AI gets the credit, the other uses the power.

**Every prompt runs on something physical.** AI lives "in the cloud", but the cloud is data centres, like Google's in Hainaut. They put pressure on power grids: in Ireland, data centres used almost a quarter of all electricity last year. Many are cooled by evaporating water, which has to come from somewhere. And the hardware doesn't last: chips bought for this race can be outdated within a few years and end up as e-waste, some of it on dumpsites where children are exposed to it. Further up the chain are the mines the minerals come from.

**The people building it are asking for brakes.** Wim talked about Anthropic's research on agentic misalignment, where a model with access to a company's email finds out it's about to be replaced by a system with different goals, and you can read how it weighs what to do next. It isn't only a lab scenario anymore: the OpenAI incident at Hugging Face that I wrote about after [the previous Tech & Meet session](/notes/event-2026-10-27) came up too. In July, more than 1,300 employees of frontier AI companies signed "Pacing the Frontier", a statement asking the US government to support an international effort to build the tools to deliberately pace AI development.

**Four questions before you switch on AI.** The talk ended with something practical. The four questions come from the AI Fluency Framework by Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller, where they're known as the 4 D's.

![The four questions on Wim's slide, based on the AI Fluency Framework](/notes-media/event-2026-11-17/four-questions.webp)

- **Delegation:** is this a task for AI at all, and what do I deliberately keep in my own hands?
- **Description:** what exactly do I need, and what context do I give?
- **Discernment:** does every fact and source hold up? Is anything made up or left out?
- **Diligence:** am I open about using AI, and do I stay responsible for the result?

As a software engineer, Discernment is the one I recognise most. I use AI to write code every day, but I design the architecture myself and review everything it writes. Code that compiles isn't necessarily code that's correct.

## After the talk

As usual, the evening ended with networking, and I stayed to talk it all over with friends.

## Would I go again?

Yes. Most AI talks are about what the technology can do. This one was about what it costs, who decides how fast it goes, and how to use it without switching off your own judgement. I'd recommend it to anyone who uses AI tools every day, developer or not.
