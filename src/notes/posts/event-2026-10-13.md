> "Your data only shows whether the people who got a loan paid it back. What do you know about the people you rejected?"

That question from Olivier Sustronck stayed with me after his Tech & Meet session on the EU AI Act, an evening in building A of the Howest campus in Bruges.

## The event

Olivier, presenting on behalf of Franklin, built the whole evening around one case: a bank where a chatbot collects information and an AI model gives every credit application a red or green score. Is it allowed? Yes. But it's high-risk, and from there he walked through what that means for everyone in the chain. The software company that builds the model is the provider. It has to keep technical documentation for ten years, pass a conformity assessment and register the system in an EU database. The bank is the deployer. It has to follow the instructions, keep humans in control, keep logs for at least six months and tell customers that AI is involved.

More legal than technical, and that was the point. Most of the obligations end up on a developer's desk.

## What I took away

**Good data is more than correct data.** Six questions: is it accurate, complete, current, consistent, representative and relevant? A model trained only on approved loans has never seen the people it turned down, so it can never learn whether it was wrong about them.

**Deleting a sensitive column doesn't delete the bias.** Origin was nowhere in the bank's data, yet approval rates ranged from 86% on one street to 22% on another. An address can do the work of the field you removed. That's proxy bias, and it's how redlining resurfaces as an algorithm.

**The AI Act doesn't replace the GDPR.** As soon as a model processes personal data, both apply.

## Did I know this already?

Partly. The legal framework came up in our IT law course, but this session made it concrete. Following one case from data to logging beats reading a list of articles.

## What could be better

The middle part was dense. Risk categories, annexes and roles in the chain came one after another, which is a lot for one sitting. A short hands-on exercise, for example classifying the bank's system ourselves, would have made the legal part stick better.

## After the talk

## Would I go again?

Yes. What struck me most as a software engineer is that a lot of what the law asks for isn't paperwork you add afterwards: logging, human oversight, being able to explain why a score is red. It's design you build in from the start. I'd recommend this session to any developer who might one day build a system that scores people.
